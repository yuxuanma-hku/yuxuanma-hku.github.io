About

I study human-AI decision making: how people interpret, verify, and appropriately rely on the outputs of AI systems. My recent research has examined when AI outputs become unreliable and most warrant human review, and how uncertainty-aware, explainable interfaces can support calibrated reliance on AI. My current proposal asks when and how strongly an AI assistant should challenge its user, using disagreement among internal agents as a signal for well-timed epistemic friction. In the long term, I aim to pursue this line of work through an MPhil or PhD.

Current Project

Disagreement-Aware Multi-Agent Interface: Adaptive Epistemic Friction for Human-AI Decision Making

Generative AI assistants tend to continue reasoning within a user's initial framing, which can amplify rather than correct a mistaken judgment. This proposal designs an interface in which the user appears to interact with a single agent while role-specialized internal agents (Evidence, Skeptic, Counterfactual, and Risk) reason in parallel; an Interaction Arbiter uses their degree of disagreement, task stakes, and user confidence to decide when and how strongly to challenge the user. A controlled online experiment will compare this adaptive epistemic friction against single-agent, always-challenge, visible-debate, and content-matched baselines, testing whether well-timed friction improves decision quality and trust calibration without significantly increasing cognitive load.
